We are grateful to both Referees for reports that were detailed, specific and,
on several points, correct where we were not. Two of the criticisms required
more than clarification, and we describe them first so that the individual
responses can be read against that background.

The loading protocol has been replaced. As the Second Referee observes, a rule
that removes molecules stochastically during repeated sweeps at fixed force
never reaches a stable state, and its results depend on the force increment. We
have adopted the standard quasi-static fiber-bundle scheme with quenched
disorder, keeping the physical content of Eq.~(4) and the coordination-dependent
weakening it describes, and every mechanical result in the paper has been
recomputed under it, on an ensemble two hundred times larger than the original
one.

The attribution of Self-Organized Criticality has been withdrawn, and so has the
comparison of our exponents with the mean-field value $5/2$. As the First
Referee notes, the process is externally driven, the two references we cited do
not support the claim, and the fitted range is too short to sustain it. A
complete Clauset-type analysis of $6.1\times10^{7}$ cascades rejects the pure
power law in 48 of 50 conditions. The revised manuscript reports the shape of
the distributions, an intrinsic cutoff and its invariances, and no exponent.

Five further responses withdraw or replace claims of the submitted version:
Eq.~(5) and its parameters are gone, the quantity $\Psi$ and its figure are
gone, the ``quantitative bridge'' between fractal dimension and failure
statistics is gone, the biological extrapolations in the conclusion are gone,
and the interpretation of $\gamma$ as a crossover in load-sharing universality
is gone. Where a claim was removed, we say below what replaced it and what
measurement supports the replacement.

In the responses, quotations in italics are the text of the revised manuscript;
in the manuscript itself all revised passages are marked in blue.

\section*{\LARGE Response to Referee 1}


\subsection*{Point 1 (R1-1): Physical grounding of the surface-diffusion parameter \(T_s\)}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  \(T_s\) controls the number of post-attachment lateral diffusion steps and
  therefore governs the ratio between the molecular relaxation timescale and the
  fibril growth rate. In real type I collagen fibrillogenesis, this ratio is
  modulated by temperature, collagen concentration, pH, and ionic strength, all
  experimentally accessible quantities. The authors acknowledge that \(T_s\) has
  no direct mapping to biochemical conditions (e.g., pH), yet proceed to
  speculate in the concluding paragraph about evolutionary fine-tuning of fibril
  compactness and its role in diseases such as pulmonary emphysema and aortic
  aneurysm. These biological inferences are unsupported without at least a
  qualitative discussion of how \(T_s\) relates to measurable physical or
  physiological variables. The manuscript would be strengthened considerably by
  such a discussion, even at the order-of-magnitude level.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We thank the Referee for the opportunity to make the physical basis of $T_s$
explicit, and we have added an order-of-magnitude interpretation to the model
description.

Because molecules are released one at a time and complete their surface
relaxation before the next is released, $T_s$ is the number of lateral
surface-diffusion trials available per deposition event, and therefore
represents the ratio between the deposition and the surface-hopping
timescales. Conditions that accelerate incorporation relative to surface
mobility correspond to a smaller effective $T_s$, and in the model to more open
cross-sections.

This sequential regime is not an arbitrary idealization. Parkinson et
al.~\cite{Parkinson1995} estimated that, at the critical concentration for
fibril formation \textit{in vitro} ($\approx 0.5~\mu$g/ml), and using the
measured translational diffusion coefficient of collagen, only of order ten
molecules lie close enough to a given region of the fibril surface to collide
with it within one second. Molecules therefore arrive essentially in isolation,
which is the regime our algorithm implements. The following passage has been
added to the model description:

\begin{quotation}\itshape
Because molecules are released one at a time and complete their surface
relaxation before the next is released, $T_s$ sets the number of lateral
surface-diffusion trials available per deposition event, and therefore
represents the ratio between the deposition and the surface-hopping
timescales. This sequential regime is consistent with the estimate of Parkinson
et al.\ that, at the critical concentration for fibril formation in vitro, only
of order ten collagen molecules lie close enough to a given region of the
fibril surface to collide with it within one second, so that molecules
effectively arrive in isolation. Conditions that accelerate incorporation
relative to surface mobility therefore correspond to a smaller effective $T_s$.
\end{quotation}

Calibrating $T_s$ against a single experimental variable would require
independent estimates of the incorporation and relaxation timescales, which is
beyond the scope of this work. Beyond that, the model treats fibrillogenesis as
pure physicochemical self-assembly, whereas \textit{in vivo} the process is
additionally organized by the cell through fibronectin, integrins, and minor
collagens~\cite{Kadler1996,Canty2005,Kadler2008}; this is why $T_s$ cannot admit
a one-to-one mapping to a single biochemical condition even in principle. We
have added this limitation to the manuscript, and it is quoted in full in our
response to Point 6.

We have also removed the evolutionary and disease-specific extrapolations from
the conclusion. Those were precisely the claims that required the calibration we
cannot provide; the remaining claims are internal to the model and require none.
In the concluding paragraph, the speculative passage has been replaced by:

\begin{quotation}\itshape
Within the present model, a greater opportunity for post-attachment relaxation
produces more compact fibrils with greater resistance to failure. Quantitative
connections to physicochemical assembly conditions will require experimental
estimates of molecular incorporation and rearrangement timescales, followed by
calibration against measured fibril growth and morphology.
\end{quotation}

\subsection*{Point 2 (R1-2): Attribution of Self-Organized Criticality}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  The authors claim that the rupture process exhibits SOC on the basis that the
  avalanche size distribution follows a power law. This reasoning is
  insufficient. SOC requires that a system spontaneously evolves toward a
  critical state without external tuning of a control parameter, the canonical
  example being the Bak--Tang--Wiesenfeld sandpile. In the present model, the
  external force \(F\) is continuously increased by the experimenter until
  failure occurs. The system is driven, not self-organizing.
  
  Critically, two of the papers the authors themselves cite to support the SOC
  claim (refs. [42] and [43], Zapperi et al., PRL 1997 and PRE 1999) make no
  reference to SOC. Instead, Zapperi et al. explicitly characterize the breakdown
  point as a first-order transition and draw an analogy with spinodal nucleation,
  a fundamentally different phenomenology. The correct terminology for the
  dynamics observed here is ``avalanche statistics in driven disordered
  fracture,'' not SOC.
  
  Furthermore, the power-law regime in Fig.~9(a) spans at most two orders of
  magnitude in avalanche size, a marginal range. No statistical validation of
  the power-law hypothesis is presented (e.g., maximum likelihood estimation of
  the exponent, Kolmogorov--Smirnov test, or comparison with alternative
  distributions following the methodology of Clauset et al., SIAM Review 51,
  661, 2009). The claim of scale-free behavior therefore rests on insufficiently
  rigorous grounds.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We agree with the Referee on both points, and the manuscript has been revised
accordingly.

\textbf{Terminology.} The process is externally driven and irreversible, and
avalanche statistics alone do not indicate self-organization. Every reference to
Self-Organized Criticality has been removed from the abstract, the introduction,
the results, and the conclusion, and the process is now described as avalanche
dynamics in driven disordered fracture. We also thank the Referee for pointing
out that Refs.~\cite{Zapperi1997a,Zapperi1999} do not support the original
claim; those citations have been corrected.

\textbf{Statistical analysis.} Under the revised loading protocol (Points 1 and
3 of the Second Referee) we analyzed the raw, integer-valued sizes of all
preterminal cascades --- $6.1\times10^{7}$ events over ten values of $T_s$ and
five values of the Weibull modulus $m$, that is, fifty conditions with 200
fibrils and 50 realizations each --- following Clauset, Shalizi and
Newman~\cite{Clauset2009}. For each condition the lower threshold $s_{\min}$ was
selected by minimizing the discrete Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance, the exponent
was estimated by exact discrete maximum likelihood, the pure power law was
tested by a semiparametric goodness-of-fit with 2500 synthetic replicas, and it
was compared with the four alternatives of Clauset et al.\ by normalized
likelihood ratios. \textbf{The pure power law is rejected ($p<0.1$) in 48 of the
50 conditions}; in 47 of them no synthetic replica reached the observed KS
distance. It loses the likelihood comparison against the lognormal and the
stretched exponential in 49 of 50. The distributions therefore have an intrinsic
cutoff, and we no longer describe the process as scale-free.

The cutoff is a property of the model rather than of the sample, and three
measurements establish this.

\textit{First, architecture.} Above $T_s\approx16$ the distributions are nearly
independent of it: at $m=2$ the survival functions for $T_s=16$, 128, 1024 and
8192 nearly coincide, with $P(S>5)$ between 0.037 and 0.042 and the 99th
percentile of the cascade size at 12 or 13 molecules, against $P(S>5)=0.077$ and
a 99th percentile of 35 for the most open architecture, $T_s=2$. This is panel
(a) of the revised Fig.~8.

\textit{Second, disorder.} The cutoff survives every width of the strength
distribution, although its value moves with it: at $T_s=128$, narrowing the
disorder from $m=1$ to $m=10$ lowers the fraction of single-molecule cascades
from 0.78 to 0.59 and raises the 99th percentile from 8 to 19. That dependence
is expected --- thresholds lying closer together fail together --- and it is the
same effect that makes the fitted exponent vary by nearly a full unit with $m$
(Point 3). What it does not do is remove the cutoff: over the whole grid of
fifty conditions the single-molecule fraction stays between 0.55 and 0.78, the
majority in every condition, and the 99th percentile between 8 and 43 molecules.

\textit{Third, and most important for the Referee's concern about a marginal
range, specimen size.} We describe this test in detail below, because its
conclusion can only be audited together with the object it was measured on.


\medskip
\textbf{Specimen size: tests on wide cross-sections.}


To test whether the observed cutoff reflects finite-size confinement or the
physics of the model, we evaluated the cascade statistics across concentric
windows on thick fibril geometries, from 2,400 to nearly 60,000 load-bearing
molecules --- 1.4 decades in system size --- in both a crossover architecture
($T_s=128$) and a compact one ($T_s=8192$). Because the architecture, the
disorder realizations and the rupture engine are held identical across windows,
the comparison isolates specimen size from every other variable. If the cutoff
were dictated by finite size, the 99th percentile would increase systematically
with $N$ while the fraction of single-molecule cascades and the fraction taken
by the terminal cascade would drop.

Obtaining such thick specimens required a detour, and we measured why rather
than assuming it. The simulated fibril grows in length, not in girth: over a
factor of 32 in the number of launched molecules $n_b$, the cross-sectional
radius scales as $R\propto n_b^{0.29}$. Reaching $R=100$ lattice units would
therefore require about $1.6\times10^{6}$ molecules at $T_s=2$ (43 h per fibril)
and $2.8\times10^{7}$ at $T_s=8192$ (155 days per fibril), the latter because
surface diffusion actively thins the fibril. An ensemble of such fibrils is out
of reach.

We therefore built a specimen that spends its molecules on the cross-section
instead of on the length: a cylinder with periodic boundary conditions along the
fibril axis, with the external launching of the aggregation model preserved
(uniform release in the period at fixed radius), so that lateral screening ---
the mechanism that makes the aggregate a DLA aggregate --- is unchanged. The
period, 216 lattice units, closes with both the rod length (18) and the
attachment rule; a closed tube would have destroyed the screening. Before
fracture the cylinder is cut open, restoring two free ends, so the rupture
engine, the trunk crop and the two-pass backbone extraction run unmodified. In
this geometry $R\propto n_b^{0.55}$, so about $5.6\times10^{4}$ molecules reach
$R\approx158$ --- the girth that free growth could not buy.

The construct was validated before it was used, against tolerances fixed in
advance. The number of molecules per cross-section is the control variable,
tuned to match the campaign fibrils within 2\%; only then were the remaining
quantities compared, over five seeds per condition. The mean coordination of the
periodic cylinder agrees with that of the freely grown fibril to $+0.7\%$ at
$T_s=2$, $+2.3\%$ at $T_s=128$ and $+1.4\%$ at $T_s=8192$ --- all below
$2\sigma$, against a tolerance of 5\% --- and the distribution of 0D--4D
molecular contacts agrees within 2.4 percentage points, reproducing the
migration toward 0D and 1D packing at high $T_s$. The seam of the periodic image
leaves no artifact: across twelve bands of 18 layers the mean occupancy is 299
molecules with a standard deviation of 32, and the band containing the seam sits
at $+1.06$ standard deviations.

The ladder itself is run on a \textit{single} wide cylinder, fractured through
four concentric windows, so that the comparison isolates size from the disorder
realization as well as from the architecture. The predictions of the two
competing hypotheses were written down before the large steps were run, and
because one or two realizations cannot estimate an extreme value, the single
largest cascade was excluded from the criterion in advance.

The finite-size hypothesis is refuted across all indicators: over a 25-fold
increase in the number of molecules, the fraction of unit cascades remains at
0.74--0.76, the 90th percentile at 2--3, the 99th percentile at 9--11, and the
terminal collapse takes 0.87--0.88 of the specimen. The single largest
preterminal cascade sampled in an individual realization does reach higher
values in larger volumes (up to 174 molecules at $T_s=128$), but the body and
the cutoff scale of the distribution do not move. A finite-size cutoff would
have shifted systematically with the specimen; this one is invariant.

We state the limitation of the ladder explicitly. The rupture algorithm costs
$O(N^2)$ --- the number of cascade steps and the cost of each backbone
re-extraction both grow linearly with $N$ --- so a single whole-section
realization takes about two hours, and the shape statistics at the largest
windows rest on one to five realizations. Those statistics are stable across the
realizations available, while the extreme value is not, which is precisely why
the criterion was restricted to the former before the data were seen. This test
is reported here rather than in the manuscript: it answers the Referee's concern
about the range of the data, but the periodic construct it requires is a method
of its own, and we judged that introducing it in the paper would add a second
subject to a study that already has one.

Because the range of cascade sizes above $s_{\min}$ spans less than one decade
in every condition, we do not report a power-law exponent as a physical finding,
nor do we compare one with theoretical scale-free predictions. What the data
support, and what the revised manuscript states, is the shape of the
distribution: a heavy body dominated by single-molecule events, an intrinsic
cutoff at a few tens of molecules fixed by the model dynamics, and a single
terminal cascade that removes most of the backbone. A power law with a
stretched-exponential cutoff describes the data well, and is among the
alternatives that defeat the pure power law in the likelihood comparison, but we
do not report its fitted parameters: over less than a decade they characterize
the fit and not the physics.

\subsection*{Point 3 (R1-3): Interpretation of \(\gamma>5/2\) in fiber-bundle theory}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  The authors interpret their measured exponent \(\gamma\approx2.8\) (at high
  \(T_s\)) as evidence of a regime beyond equal load sharing (ELS), signaling a
  crossover to global load-sharing universality. This interpretation is
  incorrect.
  
  In fiber-bundle models, \(\gamma=5/2\) is the exact mean-field result for ELS,
  the limiting case of maximally global stress redistribution (Hemmer \& Hansen,
  J. Appl. Mech. 59, 909, 1992; Daniels, Proc. R. Soc. London A 183, 405, 1945).
  There is no established theoretical framework that predicts or interprets
  exponents above \(5/2\) in this context.
  
  Moreover, Pradhan, Hansen \& Chakrabarti (Rev. Mod. Phys. 82, 499, 2010), also
  cited by the authors, demonstrate that within ELS, the avalanche exponent
  undergoes a crossover from \(\gamma=5/2\) (generic regime, away from failure) to
  \(\gamma=3/2\) (near the critical breakdown point). That is, as stress
  redistribution becomes maximally global near rupture, the exponent DECREASES,
  not increases. The authors' claim that \(\gamma>5/2\) signals increasingly
  global load sharing is therefore the opposite of what the established theory
  predicts.
  
  The observed deviation from \(5/2\) is more plausibly attributable to
  finite-size effects, limited ensemble statistics (10 fibrils per \(T_s\) value),
  the restricted range over which the power law is fitted, or the specific
  definition of avalanche adopted in the model. A sensitivity analysis with
  respect to the Weibull modulus \(m\) (fixed at \(m=2\) throughout, following
  Ref.~[35]) would also help assess the robustness of the exponents.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We thank the Referee for this observation, which corrects a genuine error in our
manuscript. We agree that interpreting the fitted exponent as a crossover to
global load sharing beyond $5/2$ was theoretically unfounded, and the claim has
been removed throughout the text, together with the comparison to $5/2$ itself.

Following the Referee's recommendations we expanded the ensemble from 10 to 200
fibrils (50 realizations per fibril, $10^4$ simulations per condition) and
performed the sensitivity analysis over five values of the Weibull modulus,
$m\in\{1,2,3,5,10\}$. The effective tail exponent depends strongly on the
disorder:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\hline
$m$ & $T_s=2$ (open) & $T_s=128$ (crossover) & $T_s=8192$ (compact)\\
\hline
1  & $1.87\pm0.01$ & $2.87\pm0.01$ & $2.83\pm0.01$\\
2  & $2.00\pm0.01$ & $2.55\pm0.01$ & $2.45\pm0.01$\\

This settles the interpretation. Narrower disorder (larger $m$) yields heavier
tails, because thresholds lie closer together and fail in larger cascades. An
exponent that varies by nearly a full unit with microscopic disorder at fixed
geometry cannot identify a load-sharing universality class. The value $5/2$
falls between $m=1$ and $m=2$ in the compact regime, but choosing $m$ to
reproduce a theoretical exponent would be an arbitrary \textit{a posteriori}
calibration. Moreover, while our cascade engine recovers the exact $5/2$
exponent when fed an idealized equal-load-sharing bundle (Point 1 of the Second
Referee), the fibril model couples global cross-sectional load sharing with
local coordination-dependent thresholds, and departs from idealized ELS
behavior. Since the preterminal range spans less than one decade in all
conditions and shows an intrinsic cutoff (Point 2), we no longer report fitted
exponents as physical findings; the sensitivity analysis is reported here rather
than in the paper, where the revised Fig.~8(b) shows the same result without a
fit.

On the plausible range of $m$ itself: to our knowledge no Weibull modulus has
been reported for single collagen fibrils, so we estimated one from published
strength measurements. For Quigley et al.~\cite{Quigley2018}, whose individual
fibril data are available, a maximum-likelihood Weibull fit gives $m=4.4$--$5.5$
($n=38$). Svensson et al.~\cite{Svensson2013} and Yang et
al.~\cite{Yang2012} report only means and standard deviations; through the
approximation $m\approx1.2/\mathrm{CV}$ their dispersions correspond to moduli
between 2.2 and 7.2. Our grid therefore spans the range these measurements
suggest. We note, however, that none of them was made on reconstituted fibrils,
which is the object our model represents; for that object no strength dispersion
has been measured, which is why we sweep $m$ rather than calibrate it. We retain
$m=2$ as the illustrative case in the figures.

\subsection*{Point 4 (R1-4): Cross-sectional fractal dimension and three-dimensional mechanical structure}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  The fractal dimension is measured in 2D but used to characterize a 3D
  mechanical problem. \(D_f\) is estimated from 2D cross-sectional projections
  (\(x\)-\(z\) plane), yet the backbone identification and the entire fracture
  simulation are three-dimensional. The relationship between the 2D
  cross-sectional \(D_f\) and the 3D structural properties that govern mechanical
  response is not established. The authors use the 2D \(D_f\) as a proxy for
  overall fibril compactness without justification. This connection should
  either be derived or explicitly discussed as an assumption with its
  limitations.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We agree with the Referee: a two-dimensional fractal dimension does not govern
the three-dimensional rupture process, and the revised manuscript no longer uses
$D_f$ as a proxy for mechanical response.

We have made the assumption explicit and stated its limitations. $D_f$ is now
presented strictly as an effective morphological descriptor of cross-sectional
compaction, and the quantities that govern the model are named as such: the
load-bearing area of each cross-section, $N(i)$, and the molecular coordination,
$K$. Both were already defined in the model description, both are measured
directly on the three-dimensional backbone, and every mechanical result is now
discussed in terms of them. We make no claim that $D_f$ determines any of them.

We also state the limitation of the estimate itself. The cross-sectional radius
of the simulated fibrils spans 15 to 40 lattice units, so the mass--radius
scaling covers less than one decade, and over so short a range the fitted slope
tracks the structural transition toward compactness rather than an asymptotic
scaling regime, and its value depends on the fitting window. The corresponding
passage in the revised manuscript reads:

\begin{quotation}\itshape
Here $D_f$ serves as an effective morphological descriptor of cross-sectional
compaction, while the mechanics developed below is governed by two quantities
measured directly on the three-dimensional backbone: the load-bearing area of
each cross-section, $N(i)$, and the molecular coordination, $K$. Given the
fibril radii accessible in simulation, 15 to 40 lattice units, the mass--radius
scaling spans less than a decade, so the fitted slope tracks the structural
transition toward compactness rather than an asymptotic scaling regime, and its
value depends on the fitting window.
\end{quotation}

We should report one consequence of having examined this point. Testing the
window dependence on the published fibrils, we found that a single objective
window rule shifts the measured values --- for instance, $D_f=1.90$ at $T_s=64$
and 1.95 at $T_s=128$, against the published 1.76 and 1.79 --- and moves the
saturation from $T_s\approx512$ to $T_s\approx128$. On wide test geometries the
local slope varies continuously from centre to edge at intermediate $T_s$,
indicating a core--shell crossover between a diffusion-limited aggregate and a
compact solid rather than a continuously evolving self-similar dimension. We
have not substituted these values in the manuscript: the mechanical conclusions
no longer depend on $D_f$ at all, and replacing the published numbers would
change the abstract, the figure and the conclusion without changing any result.
The limitation quoted above is what the reader needs in order to weigh the
number, and it is stated there.

\subsection*{Point 5 (R1-5): Empirical structural-mechanical connection}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  The key result of the paper, that \(D_f\) and \(\gamma\) saturate together at
  \(T_s\geq512\), is presented as if it were a derived causal relationship. In
  fact, it is a numerical correlation observed in simulation. No theoretical
  framework is provided that connects the fractal dimension of the fibril
  cross-section to the avalanche exponent of the fracture process. The statement
  that ``the fibril's fractal dimension serves as a quantitative bridge between
  structural compactness and failure statistics'' in the Conclusion overstates
  what has been demonstrated. The authors should reframe this as an empirical
  observation and discuss what theoretical framework might, in the future,
  explain such a connection.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We agree with the Referee: the simultaneous evolution of the cross-sectional
geometry and the avalanche statistics is an empirical observation within the
model, not a derived causal law. The phrase describing $D_f$ as a
``quantitative bridge'' has been removed throughout.

The submitted version rested that claim on a joint saturation of $D_f$ and
$\gamma$ above $T_s=512$. Under the revised analysis the mechanical half of that
coincidence no longer exists: the plateau in $\gamma$ was read from a power-law
fit over a range we now show to be inadequate, and we no longer report a fitted
exponent at all (Point 2). There is therefore nothing left for $D_f$ to coincide
with, and we have withdrawn the claim rather than restating it in a corrected
form.

What remains is a qualitative association within the model, and the revised text
says why it cannot be a functional relation $\gamma(D_f)$. The cascade
statistics depend strongly on the disorder parameter $m$ at fixed geometry,
whereas $D_f$ does not depend on $m$ at all; $D_f$ stops changing while the
local packing density and the mean coordination $\langle K\rangle$ continue to
increase up to $T_s=8192$; and the rupture algorithm reads only the load-bearing
area $N(i)$ and the coordination field $K$, with no access to self-similarity.

On the theoretical framework the Referee asks for, the revised conclusion states
the direction positively: turning the empirical connection into a predictive
theory would require formulating the stress redistribution through a continuous
disordered elastic medium carrying the cross-sectional porosity and the loss of
lateral contacts, which the present discrete framework leaves open.

We add, for completeness, that a theoretical relation connecting the avalanche
exponent to the fractal dimension of a conducting backbone is available in the
literature, but it is not transferable to this system: applied mechanically to
our data it would return a backbone dimension between 0.70 and 1.07, which is
incompatible with a continuous backbone in three dimensions. We therefore did
not use it.

\subsection*{Point 6 (R1-6): Molecular aspect ratio}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  The aspect ratio of model molecules (18:1) differs substantially from that of
  real collagen molecules (\(\approx 200{:}1\)). The impact of this
  simplification on the fractal dimension and packing geometry should be
  acknowledged.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We thank the Referee. The aspect ratio of the model molecules is indeed $18{:}1$,
after Parkinson et al.~\cite{Parkinson1997}, against $\approx 200{:}1$ for real
collagen monomers --- a figure we verified in the primary source, which reports
monomers of $\approx 300$~nm length and $\approx 1.5$~nm diameter, ``an aspect
ratio of close to 200''~\cite{Buehler2008b}. We have expanded the paragraph on
the idealizations of the model, which now reads (added text in blue in the
revised manuscript):

\begin{quotation}\itshape
Our coarse-grained framework represents collagen molecules as rigid rods with
restricted movement and no rotational diffusion. The rods have an aspect ratio
of $18{:}1$, following Parkinson et al., substantially smaller than the
$\approx 200{:}1$ ratio of real collagen monomers. While a fixed aspect ratio
ensures a consistent geometry across all $T_s$, this simplification and the
absence of rotational diffusion may quantitatively affect the coordination $K$,
the load-bearing area $N(i)$, and the fractal dimension $D_f$. Our findings
therefore capture the qualitative structural and mechanical trends of the model
rather than exact quantitative predictions for biological fibrils.

Intermolecular interactions are simplified via $T_s$, which lacks a direct
mapping to interaction changes induced by biochemical conditions such as pH.
Calibrating $T_s$ against a specific experimental variable would require
independent estimates of the incorporation and relaxation timescales, and
remains beyond our scope. Furthermore, while DLA captures the stochastic
character of molecular accretion, biological fibrillogenesis is an actively
regulated, energy-dependent process: collagen molecules first self-assemble into
microfibrils---typically containing five molecules---which are then actively
positioned and concatenated by the cell to form the growing fibril, with
fibronectin, integrins, and minor collagens organizing the process. This is a
further reason to read $T_s$ as an effective control parameter rather than as a
proxy for a single biochemical condition. Fracture is implemented as a
probabilistic model, omitting detailed force transmission and internal
bond-breaking mechanics. Consequently, the model does not produce
stress--strain curves or elastic moduli. Within these limits, the framework
captures the preterminal cascade dynamics reported above and an empirical
association between structural compactness and mechanical resilience. Within the
model that resilience is driven by the local load-bearing area $N(i)$ and the
molecular coordination $K$, so structural compaction strengthens the fibril
without requiring a self-similar load-transfer mechanism; consistently, the
cascade statistics respond to the width of the strength distribution at fixed
geometry.
\end{quotation}

The effect of the aspect ratio on $K$, $N(i)$ and $D_f$ is stated as a caveat
and not as a measured result: we have not quantified it, and we say so.

\subsection*{Point 1 (R2-1): Loading protocol and stability of the system}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  My main concern is the loading protocol. Equation (4) defines the probability
  of failure. As long as fibers carry load, this probability remains finite.
  This seems to imply that, at any finite load, the bundle would fail after a
  finite time, with a characteristic time depending on the load level. The
  authors state that during a sweep through the system, each fiber is given a
  chance to fail according to Eq.~(4), and if no failure occurs, the external
  load is increased. This procedure appears somewhat artificial. If I understand
  the model correctly, the bundle is not fully relaxed after a sweep: failure
  could continue in a subsequent sweep at the same load level. Moreover, fiber
  removal increases (\(\sigma_M\)) and decreases (\(K\)), both of which increase
  the rupture probability (\(P_R\)). Thus, the system does not seem to be in a
  stable state when the external load is further increased. The authors should
  clarify this point in the manuscript.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We thank the Referee for this observation, which identifies a genuine defect of
the original loading rule, and we have replaced the rule rather than defended
it.

The Referee's own argument settles the matter. A sweep in which no molecule
fails does not establish a stable state, because further sweeps at the same
force would continue to remove molecules, and every removal raises $\sigma_M$
and lowers $K$, as the Referee notes. It follows that the smaller the force
increment, the more sweeps are performed at each level, the more molecules are
removed before the force advances, and the lower the rupture force that is
measured. The rule therefore has no quasi-static limit, and what it measured was
a property of the loading schedule rather than of the fibril.

We have replaced the protocol by the standard quasi-static fiber-bundle scheme
with quenched disorder, keeping the physical content of Eq.~(4). Each molecule
$i$ draws once, before loading, a strength variable $X_i\in[0,1]$ with cumulative
distribution $P(X\le x)=x^{m}$, and fails when its mean stress exceeds the
threshold $\sigma^{\mathrm{th}}_i = K_i\sigma_c X_i$, where $K_i$ is its current
coordination. Under this rule the probability that a molecule of coordination
$K_i$ has failed at stress $\sigma$ is $(\sigma/K_i\sigma_c)^{m}$, which is
Eq.~(4) reinterpreted as a strength distribution; the coordination-dependent
weakening channel of the original model is preserved unchanged.

Because $\sigma_{M,i}=Fa_i$ with $a_i=\langle 1/N(i)\rangle$ over the
cross-sections the molecule spans, each molecule has a well-defined failure
force $F^{*}_i = K_i\sigma_c X_i/a_i$. The external force is raised continuously
to the smallest $F^{*}_i$ in the system; at that fixed force every molecule
above threshold is removed, loads and coordinations are recomputed, molecules
that lost their connection to both ends are removed, and the cascade repeats
until no molecule is above threshold. The cascade terminates deterministically.
There is no force increment, no sweep and no stopping criterion, so
\textbf{the state reached before each increase of the force is stable by
construction}: every remaining molecule satisfies $F^{*}_i > F$. The Referee's
objection no longer has an object.

The new protocol was validated against the exactly solvable case: fed an
equal-load-sharing bundle with uniformly distributed thresholds, the same
cascade engine reproduces the burst distribution $D(s)\sim s^{-5/2}$ of Hemmer
and Hansen, with deviations below 2\% up to $s=8$ in a run of 150 bundles of
4000 fibers. The model description, Fig.~6 and all mechanical results have been
rewritten and recomputed under this protocol, with 200 independent fibrils and
50 rupture realizations per fibril for each value of $T_s$, and for five values
of the Weibull modulus. The model has no physical time and no dwell time at
fixed load, and we do not interpret it as describing creep, fatigue or delayed
rupture.

\subsection*{Point 2 (R2-2): Localized versus equal load sharing}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  The authors discuss localized and more equal load-sharing regimes in their
  interpretation. However, in the simulations, load sharing appears to be
  determined by Eqs.~(2) and (3), which seem to impose equal load sharing within
  a cross section. If I understand correctly, information about the local
  structure enters only through \(K\) in the rupture probability. The authors
  should discuss more clearly in what precise sense the model realizes localized
  or equal-load-sharing behavior.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We thank the Referee for identifying this ambiguity. The Referee's reading of
the model is exactly right, and the original interpretation --- comparing fitted
values of $\gamma$ with fiber-bundle exponents and describing their variation
with $T_s$ as evidence of increasingly global load sharing --- was not supported
by the stress-redistribution rule actually implemented. Numerical proximity
between a fitted slope and a theoretical exponent does not establish a common
load-sharing mechanism or universality class. That interpretation has been
removed, and we no longer assign the model to either class.

The revised text states the mechanism positively. Equation~(2) distributes the
applied force uniformly among all load-bearing molecular segments within each
cross-section. When a molecule is removed, $N(i)$ decreases only in the
cross-sections it previously occupied, and the corresponding stress increase is
shared uniformly among the remaining segments there. Local structure enters
through the failure threshold: under the revised protocol molecule $i$ fails at
the external force $F^{*}_i = K_i\sigma_c X_i/a_i$, which contains two distinct
channels. The global one acts through $a_i=\langle 1/N(i)\rangle$, the mean
inverse occupancy of the cross-sections the molecule spans: any removal that
lowers $N(i)$ raises the stress on every molecule crossing that layer,
irrespective of distance. The local one acts through the current coordination
$K_i$: a removal lowers the threshold of its lateral neighbours only.

The model is therefore a hybrid, and the revised manuscript says so in those
terms. Two features of the construction set it apart from the idealized limits.
Stress is redistributed without any distance-dependent elastic kernel, so the
coordination channel carries the only local information; and $N(i)$ varies along
the fibril while molecules span different sets of cross-sections, so no single
globally shared stress governs the bundle. It combines features of equal and
local load sharing without mapping onto either limit.

Consistently with our response to Point 3 below, the primary statistical
observable is now the cascade generated at a fixed force; spatially connected
clusters are retained only as a geometric damage diagnostic and do not imply a
local-load-sharing mechanism.

\subsection*{Point 3 (R2-3): Definition of avalanches}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  Since load sharing according to Eq.~(2) is global within a cross section, it
  seems that no particular stress concentration can develop around failed
  (removed) fibers in that cross section. If this interpretation is correct,
  then the definition of avalanches may require further justification. If the
  stress field is not localized around failed clusters, it is not obvious why
  avalanches should be defined as steps in the growth of connected failed
  clusters. An alternative, and possibly more natural, definition would be to
  regard an avalanche as the set of fibers that fail between two consecutive
  increments of the external force. The authors should make this aspect of the
  work clearer.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We agree with the Referee, and we have adopted the definition suggested. Under
the quasi-static protocol described in our response to Point 1, the external
force is raised only until the next molecule reaches its threshold, and at that
fixed force a deterministic cascade follows: molecules above threshold are
removed, loads and coordinations are updated, molecules that lost their load
path are removed, and the cycle repeats until the configuration is stable. The
avalanche is the total number of molecules removed in one such cascade,
irrespective of their spatial arrangement. This is the set of fibers that fail
between two consecutive increases of the external force, and it is the standard
avalanche of fiber-bundle models. It contains no free parameter: its extent is
fixed by the model, not by a force increment or by a connectivity criterion.

The Referee's reasoning about the stress field is correct for our model. The
stress released by a removed molecule is shared uniformly among the molecules
remaining in the cross-sections it occupied (Eq.~2), and the only local effect
of a removal is the loss of one contact for each of its neighbours, which lowers
their thresholds through $K$. Spatially disconnected removals in the same
cascade are nevertheless causally linked, because each was triggered by the same
force increase or by the redistribution that followed; partitioning a cascade
into connected clusters would discard that link. We therefore no longer use the
connected cluster as the avalanche. The quantity $\Psi$ and the former Fig.~8,
which were built on the cluster definition, have been removed. The decomposition
of cascades into connected clusters remains available as a diagnostic of damage
localization --- between 12\% and 25\% of the cascades, depending on $T_s$ and
$m$, consist of more than one connected cluster --- but it is not used in the
statistical analysis.

The final cascade of each realization, which eliminates the last continuous load
path and removes the remainder of the backbone in one event (between 66\% and
91\% of the molecules, depending on $T_s$ and $m$), is a single terminal event
and not part of the preterminal avalanche process; it is excluded from the
distributions analyzed in the revised section on avalanche statistics.

\subsection*{Point 4 (R2-4): Power-law statistics and SOC}

\refereelabel
\begin{refereecomment}
  The interpretation of power-law statistics in terms of self-organized
  criticality should be treated with caution. In rupture processes the system is
  gradually destroyed, and there is no healing mechanism by which failed fibers
  could recover and carry load again. Therefore, the analogy with self-organized
  critical systems may be problematic unless it is carefully qualified. I
  suggest that the authors either provide a more detailed justification for this
  interpretation or reformulate the discussion in more cautious terms.
\end{refereecomment}

\responselabel

We agree, and we have removed the interpretation in terms of self-organized
criticality throughout. The Referee's argument is in fact borne out by the data:
with no healing, the process does not maintain a critical state. The preterminal
cascade-size distribution has an intrinsic cutoff at a few tens of molecules
that does not move with the specimen size, and every realization ends in a
single terminal cascade that removes 66\% to 91\% of the backbone at once. The
revised manuscript describes this as avalanche dynamics during progressive
damage in a driven disordered system, with the statistical analysis detailed in
our response to Point 2 of the First Referee.

The completed goodness-of-fit analysis shows that the preterminal range spans
less than one decade above $s_{\min}$, so a fitted tail exponent characterizes
the fit rather than the physical process. The revised manuscript therefore
reports the empirical shape of the distributions, the intrinsic cutoff, and its
invariances across architecture and disorder, without asserting a power-law
exponent.
